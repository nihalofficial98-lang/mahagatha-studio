When comments. When Rama was born in Ayodhya, another  avatar of Vishnu was already walking the  earth.  Parashurama,  the warrior sage with the axe, who  tradition says cleared the earth of  tyrant kings 21 times.  Here is the problem.  Ravana's famous boon protected him from  gods and demons.  It did not protect him from humans.  Parashurama  was human.  So why did Vishnu need a whole new birth  for a job a living avatar could have  done?  Today we test every popular answer  against the texts.  What survives says something surprising  about how the Ramayana thinks.  Start with the boon because everything  hangs on it.  In the Balakanda, the gods run to Brahma  in despair.  Ravana is crushing the three worlds and  nobody   can touch him.  Brahma explains why.  Ravana asked to be safe from gods,  Gandharvas, Yakshas, and demons.  He never mentioned humans.  He thought they were beneath him.  So Brahma says only a human can kill   him.  Now notice what the boon does not say.  It does not say a Kshatriya must do it.  It does not say a Brahmin cannot.  Any human would do.  On paper, Parashurama qualifies.  So whatever stopped him, it was not the  boon.  Now the story that turns this puzzle  into an itch.  In the Uttarakanda, Ravana, out  conquering the world, reaches the  Narmada.  He sets a golden Shivalinga on the sandy  bank and worships it with  heaps  of flowers.  Downstream, a king is playing in the  water with his wives,  Kartavirya Arjuna, also called  Sahasrabahu, the king with a thousand  arms.  For fun, he back the whole river with  his arms.  The water surges backward and sweeps  Ravana's offerings away.  Ravana storms off to fight him and  loses.  Kartavirya binds him and carries him  home to Mahishmati as a prisoner.  The Bhagavata Purana says he caught  Ravana the way you would catch a monkey.  It took Ravana's own grandfather, the  sage Pulastya, to get him released.  Now meet the man who killed Kartavirya.  In the Bhagavata's telling, Kartavirya  seized a wish-granting cow from the  hermitage of the sage Jamadagni.  Jamadagni's son went after him alone  with an axe and cut off all thousand  arms.  That son  was Parashurama.  So the popular math goes like this.  Kartavirya beat Ravana.  Parashurama beat Kartavirya.  So Parashurama beats Ravana.  Careful though.  Think of rock, paper, scissors.  Rock beats scissors, scissors beat  paper, and paper still beats rock.  Wins do not stack like numbers.  Shaky math or not, the question stands.  Parashurama was clearly in Ravana's  league and he never went after him.   So let us test the popular  answers, one at a time.  Answer one.  Ravana was a Brahmin.  There is something to it.  Ravana's father, Vishrava, was a Brahmin  sage.  His mother came from a Rakshasa family  and Parashurama's war was against tyrant  kings of the Kshatriya class.  So, the theory goes, Ravana was never on  his list.  It is a tidy answer,  but we could not find a single verse  where Parashurama spares Ravana because  of his birth.  So this one stays a theory.  Answer two.  Parashurama had retired.  This one has real behind it.  In  the Bala Kanda, Parashurama tells his  own story.  After his father was killed, he  destroyed the warrior kings generation  after generation.  Then he gave the entire earth to the  sage Kashyapa and moved to Mount  Mahendra. And he adds a detail.  Kashyapa told him not to live in the  land he had given away.  So Parashurama does not stay on the  earth at night.   Some read this as a ban.  He could not come back, so he could not  fight Ravana.  But look at where he is standing when he  says it. He has just stopped a royal  wedding party to challenge Rama.  So it was not a cage.  He could come down when he chose.  He just had to be gone by nightfall.   Retired? Yes.  Unable? No.  Answer three.  His power passed to Rama.  This is the most dramatic scene.  Rama has just broken Shiva's great bow  and married Sita.  On the way home, Parashurama blocks the  road, axe on his shoulder.  He holds out another bow, the bow of  Vishnu himself.  String this, he says, and then we fight.  Rama has stayed polite out of respect  for his father.  Now his patience runs  out. He  takes the bow, strings it, and sets an  arrow on it.   You are a Brahmin, Rama says, so I will  not kill you.  But this arrow takes something.  Your power to travel or  the  worlds you earned through penance.  Parashurama asks him to spare his speed.  He has to be off the earth by nightfall.  Take the worlds, he says.  The arrow flies and those worlds are  gone.  And Parashurama says it plainly.  You are Vishnu himself and there is no  shame in losing to you. Later retellings  go further.  In the Adhyatma Ramayana tradition,  Vishnu had told him long before,  when I am born as Dasharatha's son, I  will take back the power I gave you. So  yes, a baton was passed.  But check the timing.  This happens after Rama's birth, after  he is grown, after his wedding.  What stopped Parashurama in all the  years before?  This answer is silent. So where does  that leave us?  With the simplest answer, hiding in  plain sight in the Balakanda.  When the gods learn that only a human  can kill Ravana, they do not go looking  for one. They go to Vishnu with a  precise request. Be born as the four  sons of King Dasharatha and kill Ravana.  The job was assigned with a name on it.  The Mahabharata says the same.  Vishnu was born as Dasharatha's son to  destroy Ravana.  Every avatar comes with a job  description.  Narasimha came for Hiranyakashipu.  Parashurama came for Kartavirya and the  tyrant kings.  Job done, he gave the earth away and  went back to his mountain.  Think of a relay race. The first runner  may still have strong legs,  but once the baton changes hands, the  race is not his to run.  Even old poets felt this gap. About 1200  years ago,  the playwright  Bhavabhuti wrote a Parashurama who warns  Ravana to leave the forest sages alone.  After losing to Rama, he hands that duty  to him.  And there is a deeper fit.  Ravana's weakness was contempt.  He did not think humans were worth  naming.  So the answer had to be a human who  lived like one.  A prince who lost his throne, lost his  wife, wept in the forest, and built an  army of monkeys and bears.  That choice had a price.  An axe would have been faster.  Rama's way cost 14 years, a stolen wife,  and a war.  But an axe would only have ended Ravana.  Rama's way answered him.  So why didn't Parashurama kill Ravana?  Not because the boon protected Ravana.  It did not.  Not because Parashurama was too weak. No  text says that.  And not as far as any verse shows,  because Ravana was a Brahmin.  His mission was finished, and this one  was never his.  Now think about this.  Being able to do something and being the  one meant to do it are two different  things.  Parashurama knew the difference.  He handed over a bow and walked back to  his mountain.  If you know a verse that answers this  question directly, put the source in the  comments.
